37 Skills for Sellers. Zero for Permits.

SuccessMetrics Team·August 29, 2026·7 min read

Salesforce and Anthropic's Claudeforce changed what enterprise software is. Here's what it means for public sector agencies — permitting, inspections, grants.

On August 26th, Salesforce and Anthropic announced Claudeforce — and buried inside the press release is a sentence that should stop every public sector CIO mid-scroll:

“For decades, enterprise software required users to manually navigate static UI to get work done. Now that agents can access the data, workflows, and rules directly, software is a system that powers every interface.”

Read that again. The screen is no longer the product.

Claude now reasons directly over live Salesforce data, and routes every action back through Salesforce so business rules are enforced. Claude runs inside the Salesforce Trust Boundary — the first model provider whose traffic stays entirely within Salesforce's own secure environment. Claude powers the Atlas Reasoning Engine, Agentforce, and Slack.

This is real, it is shipping, and open beta lands this month.

Imagine if

Imagine if a permit technician never opened a queue again.

Instead: “What's stuck this week?” — and back comes not a list, but an answer. Fourteen applications past the statutory clock. Nine waiting on the same missing structural detail. Three where the applicant already uploaded the document and nobody linked it. One that's going to become a council complaint on Thursday.

Imagine if a grants manager could ask “which awardees are at risk?” and get a reasoned answer that weighs reporting cadence, drawdown pace, prior findings, and the site visit notes someone typed into a case comment eight months ago.

Imagine if a field inspector finished a route and simply said what happened — and the condition ratings, the follow-up work order, the reinspection date, and the notice to the property owner all landed correctly, in the right objects, under the right approvals, without a single form.

Imagine if a director asked “are we meeting our service level commitments?” on the morning the board packet was due — and got the number, the trend, and the three reasons it moved.

None of that is science fiction any more. The reasoning engine to do it shipped this month.

Salesforce showed us the pattern. Now let's extend it.

Claudeforce launched with 37 prebuilt skills for sellers — meeting prep, deal health, pipeline review. And they're excellent. Not CRM prompts bolted onto an API, but capability genuinely rebuilt around how Claude reasons, acts, and generates its own interface on the fly.

That's the important part. Salesforce and Anthropic didn't just connect a model to a database. They proved a pattern: take a domain, encode how the work actually gets done, and let the reasoning engine run it — with the platform enforcing the rules on every action.

The pattern is the gift. It generalises.

So what if we extended the very same pattern to public service?

  • A. Permitting and licensing. A skill that understands a plan review cycle the way the sales skills understand a deal cycle — what's stuck, why, who's waiting on whom, and which statutory clock runs out first. See how we already encode that work in the LPI Accelerator.
  • B. Inspections and asset condition. A skill that turns what an inspector saw into a rated condition, a follow-up work order, a reinspection date and a notice to the owner — correctly filed, properly approved, first time. That's the domain behind the Field Inspection Accelerator.
  • C. Grants and funding. A skill that reads an award portfolio the way a CRO reads a pipeline — drawdown pace, reporting cadence, prior findings, risk — and tells a programme officer where to look before the auditor does. We build that into public-sector Salesforce delivery.
  • D. Casework and constituent service. A skill that carries the whole history of a person's dealings with an agency into every interaction, so nobody has to tell their story for the fourth time.

Same architecture. Same trust boundary. Same governed actions. Different domain.

The sales skills were the proof of concept for the entire agentic enterprise. Public service is simply the next domain to encode — and the agencies that encode it first won't be modestly better than the ones that don't. They'll be operating a different kind of organisation.

And the part nobody's saying out loud

When the interface disappears, so does your safety net.

For twenty years, the page layout was the guardrail. If a caseworker shouldn't see a field, you left it off the screen. If an action needed approval, you hid the button. Messy underneath? Nobody noticed, because nobody could get there.

An agent can get there.

An agent reasons over what your permissions allow, not what your screens show. It follows your data model wherever it actually leads — including into the four overlapping custom objects three vendors left behind, the 400 fields nobody labelled, and the validation rule that was quietly bypassed in 2021.

Point a frontier model at a clean, well-governed org and you get something close to magic.

Point it at an average one and you get confident, fluent, well-written answers that are wrong — delivered at machine speed, to constituents, with your agency's name on them.

The orgs that win here are not the ones that buy the licence first. They're the ones whose data model, access model, and business rules were built to be reasoned over.

Most aren't. Yet.

Welcome to the agentic agency

This is the shift: from software your staff operate, to a system that reasons about the work and acts on it — with the rules, records, and accountability that public service demands still fully intact.

Not staff replaced. Staff released — from the queue, the form, the re-keying, the status call, the “let me look that up and get back to you.”

The technology arrived this month. The question is whether your agency is shaped to receive it.
